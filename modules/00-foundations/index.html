Module 0

Foundations

The high-school maths everything else rests on. Ten ideas, each taught from a picture first, then the symbols, then where it shows up inside a language model.

Terms with a dotted underline are a tap or hover away from a plain-language and a mathematical definition.

Compact card · 1 of 10

Variables and expressions

A letter is a labelled empty box that a number can go into.

01 Meaning

Imagine a box with a label on it and nothing inside yet. That is a variable: a name for a number we have not picked. Put 4 in the box and every place the label appears means 4. Put 10 in and it means 10.

An expression is a recipe that uses the box. Our example is “three copies of the box, plus 2”. We write it 3x+2. When a number sits next to a letter, the multiplication sign is hidden: 3x means 3 × x.

02 Picture

Drag the slider to change the number in the box. The three blue bars and the ochre block are the recipe; the navy bar is the answer.

03 Real life

  • A taxi that charges a $2 pick-up fee plus $3 per kilometre: the fare depends on the kilometres, so kilometres get a letter.
  • A recipe that lists three eggs per guest plus two spare: the guest count is the empty box.
  • A phone plan with a fixed fee plus a price per gigabyte.

Recognition rule. If a quantity depends on something you can change, give that something a letter.

Spot it. A gym charges a $20 sign-up fee plus $15 for each month you stay. Which part is the variable?

04 Worked example

Predict, then reveal

Replace x with 5, do the multiplication first, then the addition.

05 Why and when

Without variables you would write a separate sentence for every case: “3 times 4, plus 2”, “3 times 5, plus 2”, and so on. A variable lets one statement cover every case at once.

Use one whenever a rule should hold for many numbers. If you only ever need one specific number, just write the number.

06 How to arrive at it

◆ Convention

Any mark could stand for the box: a square, a star, the letter k. We could have chosen differently, and the maths would not change. The habit is to use letters from the end of the alphabet (x, y, z) for quantities that vary and letters from the start (a, b, c) for fixed numbers we have not specified.

07 In an LLM

Every number a language model learns is a variable, called a parameter, until training fixes its value. The model is a very large expression made of those variables. GPT-3 (2020) has 175 billion of them (Brown et al., 2020).

Simplified. A real model's expression is enormously bigger than 3x + 2, but it is built from the same pieces: numbers, variables, multiplication and addition (plus a few other operations we meet later).

Teach it

Everyday analogy

A form with blanks: “Dear ____”. The blank stands for whatever name you fill in. Where it breaks down: a blank on a form is filled once, but a letter in maths can be filled with different numbers to see what happens, and the same letter means the same number everywhere in one problem.

Common misconception

“x always means the same number.” It does not. x is a placeholder; its value is whatever the problem says or whatever we choose for the moment.

Further reading: MML Ch 1 (motivation) for how these pieces become machine learning.

Equations

An equation links quantities. It is a rule that must stay true, not an instruction to calculate from left to right.

01 Meaning

Pour 4 litres of water into two glasses. However you split it, what is in the first glass plus what is in the second glass is 4 litres. That sentence is an equation: a statement that two expressions have the same value.

Give the amounts letters, x and y, and the rule becomes

x + y = 4

Here x and y are unknowns. Notice what the equation does not say: it does not tell you x or y. It only says how they are tied together. Many pairs work, such as 1 and 3, or 0 and 4. Each pair that makes it true is a solution.

02 Picture

A pair (x, y) is a point on a sheet, described by its coordinates. Plot every solution of x + y = 4 and the points fall on a straight line. Slide x to walk along it. Then add a second equation and watch where the two lines meet.

03 Real life

  • Splitting 4 litres of water between two glasses (volume is conserved).
  • A 24-hour day divided between sleep and everything else.
  • A fixed budget shared between two purchases: spend more on one and you must spend less on the other.
  • Mass in a sealed box: the mass of the gas plus the mass of the liquid stays the same while one turns into the other.

Recognition rule. If something is fixed in total while its parts can trade off against each other, think “x + y = total”. If a second, independent fact is also known, you can pin the parts down.

Spot it. A 60-minute class is split between listening (t minutes) and practising (p minutes). Which equation captures the split?

04 Worked example

Two facts about the same pair: they add to 4, and the first exceeds the second by 2. Find the pair.

Predict, then reveal

Put 1 where x is, then ask what must be added to 1 to reach 4.

05 Why and when

An equation lets you state a relationship before you know the numbers, then use it to find the missing ones. Without it you would be guessing pairs one after another.

The counting rule. One equation with two unknowns leaves a whole line of solutions. A second equation usually pins down a single point. Two things can go wrong, and the widget shows both: the second line can be parallel, so nothing satisfies both, or it can be the first line rescaled, so it adds no new information.

An equation does not fit a relationship that is “at most” or “at least”. Those describe a region, not a line.

06 How to arrive at it

✓ Derived

  1. The requirement. Say “the two parts always total 4” without choosing numbers.
  2. Obvious candidates. List the pairs (0 and 4, 1 and 3, …). Or describe it in a sentence.
  3. Why they fail. A list never ends once fractions are allowed, and a sentence cannot be calculated with.

Before you open the next part, predict: how many equations do you think you need to pin down both x and y?

I have made my prediction: show the reasoning

Each equation removes one degree of freedom. Two unknowns start with two free choices. One equation ties them, leaving one free choice (slide x and y follows). A second independent equation removes the last one. So you need as many independent equations as unknowns. That count is forced by the requirement, which is why this card is tagged Derived. The symbol “=” itself is notation we agree on.

07 In an LLM

  • Probabilities add to 1. A language model gives every token in its vocabulary a probability, and together they total 1. If three tokens have 0.5, 0.25 and 0.125, the rest of the vocabulary shares the remaining …. This is the same “parts of a fixed total” idea as x + y = 4. The softmax function that produces the probabilities is built so that they add to 1.
  • The context window is a total. The prompt plus the reply must fit within the context window. GPT-3 (2020) had a window of 2,048 tokens (Brown et al., 2020); with a 1,500-token prompt, … tokens remain for the reply.
  • A neuron's boundary is a line. For a single unit with two inputs, the set where its weighted sum equals zero, w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b = 0, is a line. See the slope card for the pieces.

Simplified. The third point describes a single unit in a small classifier. A real layer has thousands of inputs, so its boundary is a flat sheet in a very high-dimensional space, and the units in a transformer are not read as individual boundaries.

Explain it back

One equation only ties x and y together, so choosing x leaves y fixed but x free: a whole line of pairs works. A second independent equation is another tie, which leaves no free choice and picks out a single pair.

Teach it

60-second explanation

“An equation is a promise that two things are equal. Say two glasses hold 4 litres together. If I tell you the first glass has 1 litre, you know the second has 3. But if I only tell you the total, there are endless ways to split it. Draw them all and you get a straight line. A second fact, like ‘the first has 2 more than the second’, draws a second line, and the glasses can only be where the lines cross.”

Everyday analogy

A balanced see-saw: whatever you do to one side, you must do to the other. Where it breaks down: a see-saw has two sides to balance, but an equation can have unknowns on both sides, and a see-saw does not show you the whole family of solutions.

Whiteboard sequence

  1. Draw two glasses and write 4 L above them.
  2. Write x and y under the glasses, then x + y = 4.
  3. List three pairs that work.
  4. Plot the pairs and join them into a line.
  5. Draw a second line and circle the crossing.

Questions learners ask

Is “=” an instruction to calculate? No. It states that both sides are the same value. Calculating is one way to use it.

Can x and y be negative or fractions? Yes, unless the problem says otherwise. That is why the line continues past the axes.

Why do two equations sometimes fail? If they say contradictory things (parallel lines) there is no pair; if one repeats the other, there is no new information.

Common misconception

“The answer must be on the right of the equals sign.” The equals sign only says both sides match; the unknown can be anywhere.

Further reading: MML Ch 2.3 (solving systems of linear equations) for the full treatment, coming in Module 1.

Functions and graphs

A function is a rule that gives exactly one output for each allowed input. A graph makes the rule visible.

01 Meaning

Think of a vending machine. Press the same button and you always get the same snack. The button you press is the input; the snack that drops is the output. A function is any rule that behaves like that: one input in, exactly one output out, the same every time.

We write the machine as a name and a recipe. For “double it, then add 1”:

f(x) = 2x + 1

Read f(x) as “the output of f when the input is x”. The letter f is just the machine's name.

02 Picture

A graph draws the machine's whole behaviour: for each input, mark a point at the height of its output. Choose a rule, then slide the input and watch the point trace the curve.

03 Real life

  • A price list: item in, price out.
  • A speedometer: moment in time in, speed out.
  • A taxi meter: distance in, fare out.
  • A thermostat dial: setting in, target temperature out.

Recognition rule. If asking the same question twice must give the same answer, and the answer depends on something you can vary, think “function”. If the same input could give different outputs, it is not one.

Spot it. Which of these is a function from the first thing to the second?

04 Worked example

Predict, then reveal

Replace x with 5 and follow the recipe: double, then add 1.

05 Why and when

Naming a rule lets us treat “how the output depends on the input” as one object that we can draw, compare, combine and improve. Without the idea you would have only a pile of separate facts.

It fits whenever each input has a single predictable output. It does not fit a rule where the same input can give different outcomes, such as a dice roll. For that you describe a spread of possible outputs with probabilities (Module 5).

06 How to arrive at it

● Motivated

  1. The requirement. A machine you can rely on: tell it what you put in and know what comes out.
  2. Obvious candidates. A complete list of pairs; a formula; a drawing.
  3. The catch. What if an input was allowed to give two different outputs?

Predict: what goes wrong if one input can produce two outputs?

I have made my prediction: show the reasoning

You could no longer say what “the” output is, so you could not calculate with it or draw a single curve. Requiring exactly one output per input is what keeps the machine dependable. It is tagged Motivated because it is a very good fit for that need, but other notions (relations that allow several outputs) exist and are useful elsewhere.

07 In an LLM

Seen from outside, a language model is a function. Its input is the sequence of tokens so far; its output is a probability for every token in the vocabulary being the next one. Feed it the same tokens and the same settings, and the function gives the same probabilities.

Simplified. When you chat, the system then samples a token from those probabilities. That random step happens outside the function, which is why the same prompt can produce different replies. Sampling comes in Module 5.

Explain it back

A function is a rule that takes an input and gives one definite output, the same every time. If an input could give two outputs we could not say what the output is, so we could not calculate or draw it as one curve.

Teach it

60-second explanation

“A function is a vending machine for numbers. You press a button, the machine follows its rule, and out comes one result. Press the same button again, same result. The graph is just a picture of every button and what it gives: left to right the input, up and down the output.”

Everyday analogy

A recipe: ingredients in, dish out. Where it breaks down: a real recipe can turn out differently each time; a function never does.

Whiteboard sequence

  1. Draw a box with an arrow in and an arrow out.
  2. Write the rule inside: double, then add 1.
  3. Feed in 0, 1, 2 and write the outputs.
  4. Draw axes and plot the three points.
  5. Join them; ask what happens between the points.

Questions learners ask

Is f(x) the same as f times x? No. f(x) means “the output of f at x”. The brackets mean “apply”.

Can two inputs give the same output? Yes. Both 2 and −2 give 4 under “multiply it by itself”. A function only forbids one input giving two outputs.

Why draw a graph at all? Because patterns such as steepness or turning points are easier to see than to read from a table.

Common misconception

“A function must have a formula.” A lookup table or a learned model is a function too, as long as each input has one output.

Further reading: MML Ch 2 and Ch 5 introduce functions on vectors; Module 4 returns to them.

Slope and linear functions

A straight line changes by the same amount for every equal step across.

01 Meaning

Walk up a ramp. For every step you take forward, you rise by the same amount. How steep the ramp is, how much you go up per step forward, is its slope: a steady rate of change. Where the ramp starts, its height at the very beginning, is the intercept.

A linear function packs both facts into one rule:

y = mx + b

Here m is the slope (rise per step across), b is the intercept (the height at x = 0), x is the step count and y the height.

02 Picture

Change the slope and the line tilts; change the intercept and it slides up or down. The small burgundy triangle shows “run 1, rise m” at the start. The ochre dot is the intercept.

03 Real life

  • A tank that starts with some water and fills at a steady number of litres per minute.
  • A cyclist at constant speed who starts some distance down the road.
  • A taxi fare: a fixed pick-up fee plus a steady price per kilometre.
  • A road gradient: metres climbed for each hundred metres travelled.

Recognition rule. If the same step in one quantity always changes the other by the same amount, it is linear. The slope is that amount; the intercept is where you start.

Spot it. A candle is 20 cm tall and burns down 2 cm every hour. Which rule gives its height h after t hours?

04 Worked example

Predict, then reveal

How far up does it go, and how far across? Divide the first by the second.

05 Why and when

A straight line is the simplest relationship that still has a direction and a size. It is the baseline we compare other shapes to. Zoom in far enough on almost any smooth curve and it looks like a line, which is the idea behind derivatives (Module 4).

It does not fit processes where the step depends on the current size (next card), or where an effect levels off.

06 How to arrive at it

✓ Derived

  1. The requirement. Each equal step across changes the height by the same amount, m.
  2. Start. At x = 0 the height is b.
  3. Take steps. After 1 step: b + m. After 2 steps: b + m + m.

Predict: what is the height after x steps?

I have made my prediction: show the reasoning

After x steps you have added m exactly x times to b, so the height is b + x copies of m, which is mx + b. Nothing else is possible if every step adds the same amount, so the formula is forced: it is Derived.

07 In an LLM

A single artificial neuron computes a weighted sum plus a bias. With one input it is exactly this line:

z = w x + b

The weight w plays the role of the slope and the bias b plays the role of the intercept. With many inputs the same pattern becomes a sum of weight times input over all the inputs, plus the bias. Transformer layers are built from such weighted sums (Vaswani et al., 2017).

Simplified. Most units apply an activation function after this step, which bends the output. We meet that in Module 10. “Neuron” is a metaphor; the unit is just this formula.

Explain it back

m is how much the output rises for each one-step increase in the input: the steepness and direction. b is where the line starts, the output when the input is zero.

Teach it

60-second explanation

“Imagine a ramp. How far up it starts is b. How steep it is is m: each step forward lifts you by m. After x steps forward you have gone up x times m, plus the b you started with. That is y = mx + b. A neuron in an AI is the same recipe with weights and a bias.”

Everyday analogy

A taxi fare: pick-up fee plus a price per kilometre. Where it breaks down: real fares often have minimums or caps, which bend the line.

Whiteboard sequence

  1. Draw axes and mark a dot at height b on the vertical axis.
  2. Draw one step across and one rise of m.
  3. Repeat the step three times.
  4. Join the points into a line.
  5. Write y = mx + b and label each part.

Questions learners ask

What if the slope is negative? The line goes down as you move right.

What if the slope is zero? A flat line: the output never changes.

Why is it called linear? Because the graph is a line.

Common misconception

“Slope is the angle.” Slope is rise over run, a ratio. Doubling the slope does not double the angle.

Further reading: MML Ch 2.7 (linear mappings) for the generalisation to many inputs.

Exponents, growth and decay

Repeated multiplication by the same number describes change proportional to size.

01 Meaning

Fold a sheet of paper in half, then in half again. Each fold doubles the thickness. Adding the same amount each time makes steady progress; multiplying by the same number each time makes progress that speeds up (or slows down) with the size.

Writing “2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2” gets tiresome, so we count the copies with a small raised number, the exponent. The number being multiplied is the base:

2⁵ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2

If a quantity starts at a and is multiplied by a factor r at every step, after n steps it is

a rⁿ

When r is bigger than 1 this is growth. When r is between 0 and 1 it is decay.

02 Picture

Burgundy bars multiply by the factor at every step. Ochre diamonds show what you would get by adding the first step's change every time instead. Raise the number of steps and watch which one runs away.

03 Real life

  • A lily patch that doubles its coverage of a pond each day.
  • Savings with compound interest: each year's interest is added to the base for next year's.
  • A hot drink cooling toward room temperature: the gap shrinks by about the same fraction each minute.
  • A radioactive material halving over each half-life.

Recognition rule. If each step multiplies by the same factor, think exponent. If each step adds the same amount, think line. A curve that gets steeper as it grows, or flatter as it shrinks, is a sign of multiplying.

Spot it. A pond's lily patch covers twice as much surface each day. How does the coverage change?

04 Worked example

Predict, then reveal

Write the sequence one step at a time: 1, then double it, then double again, and so on.

05 Why and when

The exponent compresses “multiply this many times” into one symbol and exposes how startlingly fast repeated multiplication runs away, or fades. Without it, long products are error-prone and the speed is hidden.

It fits changes proportional to current size. It does not fit steady changes (use a line) or processes that level off (those need other curves).

06 How to arrive at it

✓ Derived

  1. The requirement. The change at each step should be in proportion to the current amount.
  2. Obvious candidate. Add a fixed amount each step.
  3. Why it fails. A fixed addition ignores the size: 10 and 10,000 would grow by the same amount.

Predict: what operation makes the change scale with the size?

I have made my prediction: show the reasoning

Multiplying by a fixed factor r changes an amount of size A by A × (r − 1), which is proportional to A. Repeating that n times from a start of a gives a·r·r·…·r, which we abbreviate a·rⁿ. The form is forced by the requirement, so it is Derived.

07 In an LLM

With a vocabulary of V tokens, the number of possible sequences of n tokens is Vⁿ. In the original Transformer's English–German translation experiment, the shared vocabulary was about 37,000 tokens; the English–French experiment used a 32,000 word-piece vocabulary (Vaswani et al., 2017). Even a sequence of just two tokens already has … possibilities.

That explosion is why a language model cannot store a table of answers for every sequence. It computes a probability from a formula instead.

Simplified. This is a counting argument, not a description of how any particular model works. Many sequences are nonsense, and models never visit most of them.

Explain it back

Adding a fixed amount changes everything by the same step, so the graph is a line. Multiplying by a factor changes things in proportion to their size, so the steps get bigger as the amount grows (or smaller as it shrinks). The exponent counts how many times the factor has been multiplied in.

Teach it

60-second explanation

“Adding is like walking: the same distance every step. Multiplying is like a snowball rolling downhill: the bigger it gets, the more snow it picks up. We write ‘multiply 5 times’ as a small 5 above the number. Doubling just ten times from 1 gets you past a thousand.”

Everyday analogy

Folding paper. Where it breaks down: real paper cannot be folded more than a handful of times, so the pattern stops being physical long before the formula does.

Whiteboard sequence

  1. Write 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 in a row and arrows labelled ×2.
  2. Below it write 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 with arrows labelled +1.
  3. Draw both as bars.
  4. Label the doubling bars 2⁰ … 2⁴.
  5. Ask: which one is ahead after 10 steps?

Questions learners ask

What is 2⁰? 1: zero multiplications leaves the start untouched.

Can an exponent be negative? Yes; it means dividing instead of multiplying. We meet that with logarithms.

Why does decay never reach zero? Halving leaves half of what remains, which is never nothing.

Common misconception

“2⁵ means 2 × 5.” It means 2 multiplied by itself five times: 32, not 10.

Further reading: MML Ch 1 and the opening of Ch 5; logarithms (the inverse of this idea) come next in this module.

Logarithms

A logarithm counts how many times you multiplied.

01 Meaning

Start at 1 and keep multiplying by 2: 1, 2, 4, 8. How many multiplications does it take to reach 8? Three. That count, three, is the logarithm of 8 for the base 2. The base is the number you keep multiplying by.

We write it log₂ 8 = 3. In general, log_b x = k means “multiply 1 by b exactly k times to get x”, which is the same as bᵏ = x.

The count does not have to be whole. 50 sits between 10 and 100, so its base-10 logarithm sits between 1 and 2 (about …).

The useful consequence: gluing two runs of multiplications end to end adds their counts. So the logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms.

log(ab) = log a + log b

02 Picture

Move the slider to multiply more times. The boxes show 1 multiplied by the base again and again (k means thousand, M million). On the ordinary ruler, position is proportional to the number, so the dot barely leaves the start. On the log ruler, position is proportional to the number of steps, so every multiplication moves the dot the same distance and one picture holds numbers of very different sizes.

03 Real life

  • Earthquake magnitude. Each whole step up the scale means the ground moves about … times as far (amplitude on a seismograph), but releases roughly 30 times as much energy (about … under the classic 1.5 relation; USGS also gives a newer estimate near 27), not 10. Two steps: … times the amplitude and about … times the energy under the classic relation.
  • Decibels. A rise of 10 dB is … times the power. A rise of 20 dB is … times the amplitude (of pressure or voltage), because power goes as amplitude squared, which makes 20 dB also … times the power. Power doubling is about … dB.
  • pH. Each step down is roughly ten times the effective concentration of hydrogen ions (strictly, their activity). Pure water at room temperature sits near pH ….
  • Octaves. Each octave doubles the frequency. From a note at 110 Hz to one at 880 Hz is … octaves.
  • Bits. Picking one of 8 equally likely options takes … yes-or-no questions.

Recognition rule. If equal steps on a scale mean equal multiplications, not equal additions, the scale is logarithmic.

A caution: “human perception is logarithmic” is only a rough rule. Loudness and pitch follow ratios fairly well over a middle range, but not exactly, and other fits, such as power laws, describe some senses better. Treat it as a reason log scales are handy, not a law.

Spot it. A news report says a magnitude 7 earthquake struck, after a magnitude 6 earlier in the week. Compared with the magnitude 6, how much larger is the ground motion (amplitude) of the magnitude 7?

04 Worked example

Predict, then reveal

Write the doublings out: 1, 2, 4 … and count the arrows until you land on 32.

05 Why and when

Numbers made by repeated multiplication span enormous ranges: the quietest and loudest sounds people can comfortably hear span about 120–130 dB SPL (decibels of sound pressure level, relative to 20 µPa). At 120 dB that is a power ratio of about …. Logarithms squeeze that range into small, evenly spaced numbers and turn multiplying into adding, which is far easier to handle.

They fit quantities that grow or shrink by ratios. They do not fit zero or negative numbers (no number of multiplications gives them), and they are the wrong tool when changes are additive.

06 How to arrive at it

✓ Derived ◆ Convention

  1. The requirement. We want a number that turns multiplying into adding: f(a × b) = f(a) + f(b).
  2. Obvious candidate. Use the number itself, f(x) = x.
  3. Why it fails. 3 × 4 = 12, but 3 + 4 = 7, not 12.

Predict: what could you count about a product that adds up when you multiply?

I have made my prediction: show the reasoning

Count the multiplications by the base. Multiplying b^j by b^k lays j runs and k runs end to end, giving b^(j+k), so the counts add. The idea is forced by the requirement (Derived). The choice of base is a Convention: another base only multiplies every logarithm by the same constant, which is why base 2 (bits), base 10 and base e (the natural logarithm) can be used interchangeably up to units.

07 In an LLM

A language model assigns a probability to each next token, and the probability of a whole sequence is the product of the individual probabilities (Module 5). Multiply 400 probabilities of 0.01 and the exact answer is a 1 with 800 zeros after the decimal point; ordinary double-precision arithmetic has nothing that small and returns …. Add the natural logs instead and the total is a comfortable …. This is why models and their training code work with log-probabilities.

Logs also set the units of the usual quality measures. If a model spreads its probability evenly over 4 tokens, the surprise at each is ln 4 = … nats, or … bits. The perplexity is e raised to the average surprise in nats, which comes to …: as unsure as a choice among four equally likely tokens.

Simplified. Real per-token probabilities vary; 0.01 and a uniform spread over four tokens are chosen so the arithmetic is visible. Perplexity is defined as the exponential of the average negative log-probability; using bits and 2 raised to the power gives the same number.

Explain it back

A logarithm counts how many times you multiply by the base to reach a number. Multiplying two numbers joins their runs of multiplications end to end, so the counts add. That is why log(ab) = log a + log b, and why logarithms turn long products into short sums.

Teach it

60-second explanation

“Start at 1 and keep multiplying by 10. 10, 100, 1,000. The logarithm just counts the steps: three. It turns a huge range into small evenly spaced numbers, which is why earthquakes, sound and acidity are reported on log scales. And since gluing two runs of tens together adds the counts, multiplying numbers becomes adding their logs.”

Everyday analogy

Counting the zeros in a number: 1,000 has three, 1,000,000 has six. Where it breaks down: it works only for exact powers of ten; real logarithms also handle numbers like 50.

Whiteboard sequence

  1. Draw a row of boxes 1, 10, 100, 1,000 with ×10 arrows.
  2. Label the arrows 1, 2, 3 as you count them.
  3. Circle 1,000 and write log₁₀ 1000 = 3.
  4. Draw a second row of equal spacing, one box per power.
  5. Show 100 × 1,000 as two runs joined, 2 + 3 = 5.

Questions learners ask

Is a logarithm of 1 always 0? Yes: zero multiplications leave you at 1.

Why is there no log of 0? No amount of multiplying 1 by a positive number ever lands on 0.

Why does an earthquake step differ for amplitude and energy? They are different quantities. Measured amplitude is defined to grow tenfold per step, while an empirical relation between magnitude and energy says energy grows by roughly 30 times per step (101.5 ≈ 32 under the classic relation; USGS also gives a newer estimate near 27). Both are multiplications; the sizes differ.

Common misconception

“A 20 dB sound is twice as powerful as a 10 dB sound.” It is 10 times the power. Equal steps on a log scale are equal multiplications, not equal additions.

Further reading: MML Ch 1 (motivation), and the use of logarithms of probabilities in Ch 6 and 8.

Σ notation

Σ is a loop that adds: run the counter, work out each term, total them.

01 Meaning

Think of a shop receipt. To get the total you go down the lines and add each price. A sum written with the Greek capital letter Σ (sigma) is that same instruction, packed small.

Under the Σ sits a counter, called the index, and where it starts. Above it sits where the counter stops. To the right is the recipe for each term. For example:

Σ i² = 1² + 2² + 3² + 4²

It reads “add up i squared, for i from 1 to 4”.

02 Picture

Each bar is one term. Filled bars are inside the range and are added; outlined bars are skipped. Change where the counter starts and stops, or the recipe, and watch the expanded sum below.

03 Real life

  • A receipt total: one term per line.
  • Steps walked in a week: one term per day.
  • A road trip's distance: one term per leg.
  • Rainfall for a month: one term per day.

Recognition rule. If you are adding up one number for each item in a list, you are doing a Σ.

Spot it. A bike ride has four legs of lengths d₁, d₂, d₃, d₄ kilometres. Which expression is the total distance?

04 Worked example

Predict, then reveal

Write the four terms for i = 1, 2, 3, 4, then add them.

05 Why and when

Without Σ you would write “a₁ + a₂ + … + a₁₀₀₀” and hope the reader fills in the dots correctly. Σ states the rule exactly and works for any length, including a length n that is not yet known.

Use it for adding a family of terms that follow one recipe. If the terms have no pattern, just list them. If a stop value is below the start, the sum is empty and equals 0.

06 How to arrive at it

◆ Convention

The idea, a loop that adds terms, is not optional: any long sum needs some way to say “continue the pattern”. The symbols are a Convention: Σ for “sum”, i as the usual counter, start below and stop above. A book could swap them, and some do write the range after the symbol. What matters is that the rule, the start and the stop are all stated.

07 In an LLM

Two Σ's carry most of the load. The dot product of a list of weights with a list of inputs is a sum of products: with w = (2, −1, 3) and x = (4, 5, 1), it is 2·4 + (−1)·5 + 3·1 = …. A neuron computes this, and attention scores in a transformer are dot products too (Module 2).

The second is the loss. The total surprise of a text is the sum of −log₂ p over its tokens. For three tokens with probabilities ½, ¼ and ⅛ that is 1 + 2 + 3 = … bits, or … bits per token on average. Dividing by the number of tokens is the idea behind the per-token training loss for next-token prediction.

Simplified. Real training losses are usually reported in nats (natural logs) and averaged over many tokens and many texts, but the structure is the same: a sum of a per-token term.

Explain it back

The start says where the counter begins and the stop says where it ends. The expression on the right is the recipe for one term, written with the counter. Run the counter from start to stop, work out each term, then add them all.

Teach it

60-second explanation

“Σ means ‘add these up’. Underneath is a counter and where it starts, on top is where it stops, and on the right is the recipe for each term. Run the counter, work out each term, add. It is just a for-loop that keeps a running total.”

Everyday analogy

Adding up a receipt. Where it breaks down: a receipt lines are arbitrary prices; a Σ's terms come from one rule.

Whiteboard sequence

  1. Write Σ with start 1 and stop 4.
  2. Write the recipe i² on the right.
  3. Expand into 1² + 2² + 3² + 4².
  4. Work out each term.
  5. Add.

Questions learners ask

Does the counter have to be i? No, j, k or t work; it is just a label.

Can the start be 0? Yes, and in computing it often is.

What if the stop is smaller than the start? The sum is empty and equals 0.

Common misconception

“Σ i² is the same as (Σ i)².” It is not: 1 + 4 + 9 + 16 is not 10 squared.

Ratios, percentages and averages

Compare by dividing; summarise by averaging.

01 Meaning

A ratio compares two amounts by dividing: 3 cups of flour to 1 cup of water is a ratio of 3 to 1. A percentage is a ratio written out of 100: …% means 15 out of 60.

An average squeezes a list into one number. The mean shares the total out evenly; the median is the middle value of the sorted list. They answer different questions.

02 Picture

Four values stay fixed. Drag the fifth outward: the mean ▲ chases it, while the median ■ stays put.

03 Real life

  • A recipe scaled up keeps the same ratio of ingredients.
  • A 20% discount: multiply the price by 0.8.
  • Average house prices in a town: one very expensive sale drags the mean, the median hardly moves.

Recognition rule. If a number only makes sense relative to something else, it is a ratio. If one huge value is distorting a summary, check the median.

Spot it. A savings rate goes from 2% to 3%. How should it be described?

04 Worked example

Predict, then reveal

Add the three values, then divide by how many there are.

05 Why and when

Ratios and percentages let you compare things of different sizes: 30 out of 40 and 60 out of 100 can be put side by side. Averages let you describe a pile of numbers with one. Without them, comparing needs the whole raw list.

Use the mean for fairly symmetric data and when the total matters; use the median when a few extreme values would mislead. An outlier is such a value.

06 How to arrive at it

◆ Convention ● Motivated

“Out of 100” is a Convention: we could have used out of 10 or 1,000. Choosing the mean or the median is Motivated by the question: the mean when you care about the total, the median when you care about the typical case. There is no single correct average.

07 In an LLM

A training or validation loss is a mean: the per-token loss averaged over the tokens (and texts) in a batch. Benchmark scores are usually percentages: correct answers as a share of all questions. The link is direct, but the details of which tokens are averaged and how vary between training setups.

Teach it

Everyday analogy

The mean is where a see-saw balances; the median is the person in the middle of a queue. Where it breaks down: a queue has no weights, so a very tall person at the end doesn't tip anything.

Common misconception

“Up from 2% to 3% is a 1% rise.” It is one percentage point, which is a 50% rise relative to the old rate.

Further reading: MML Ch 6 (means and variances arrive properly with probability).

Sine and cosine

Two shadows of an arrow turning around a circle.

01 Meaning

Draw a circle of radius 1, the unit circle, and an arrow from its centre to a point on the rim. Turn the arrow through an angle θ from the rightward direction. Shine a light from above and from the side: the arrow's shadows on the axes are what we study.

The horizontal shadow is the cosine, cos θ. The vertical shadow is the sine, sin θ. Angles are measured in degrees (a full turn is 360°) or radians (a full turn is 2π).

02 Picture

Turn the arrow with the slider. Blue is the cosine, teal dots the sine. Watch them change sign as the arrow crosses an axis.

03 Real life

  • The height of a seat on a Ferris wheel as it turns.
  • A sled pulled by a rope at an angle: only the horizontal part pulls it forward.
  • A pure musical tone: the air pressure at one spot rises and falls like a sine over time.
  • A small swinging pendulum: its sideways position is approximately a sine over time (the approximation fails for big swings).

Recognition rule. If you need the part of a length or force that lies along a direction, think cosine; the part across it, think sine.

Spot it. A sled is pulled with a 50 N rope at 60° above the ground. How much of that force pulls it forward?

04 Worked example

Predict, then reveal

Cosine is the horizontal position on the unit circle. Where is the arrow's tip horizontally?

05 Why and when

Cosine and sine split a slanted quantity into the part along an axis and the part across it, and they describe anything that cycles. Without them, tilted arrows and repeating waves have no simple handle.

They suit rotation and oscillation; steady growth or decay needs exponents instead.

06 How to arrive at it

● Motivated ◆ Convention

In a right triangle, “adjacent over hypotenuse” gives cosine for angles up to 90°. Defining it on the unit circle is Motivated: it extends the same idea to every angle, including those past 90°, where the old triangle picture breaks. The unit of angle is a Convention: 360 degrees is a historical choice, while radians are the unit that makes later formulas simplest.

07 In an LLM

Cosine is how we compare vectors: cosine similarity of two vectors is the cosine of the angle between them, built from the dot product (Module 2).

Sine and cosine also appear directly. The original Transformer added fixed positional encodings made of sines and cosines to its token vectors, so the model can tell positions apart; for position 1, the first pair (the highest frequency, with a wavelength of 2π) is (sin 1, cos 1) = (…, …) (Vaswani et al., 2017). The paper also tried learned position vectors and reported nearly identical results.

Simplified. The full encoding uses many frequencies across the vector (with d_model = 512 in the base model). Many later models use other ways of encoding position.

Teach it

Everyday analogy

The shadow of a stick held at a slant: from above you see its horizontal part, from the side its vertical part. Where it breaks down: a real shadow has no sign, but cosine and sine can be negative.

Common misconception

“Sine and cosine are only for triangles.” They describe the position on a circle at any angle, and triangles are just the first case.

Further reading: MML Ch 3 (angles and inner products), and the Ch 1 opening for notation.

Reading mathematical notation

Symbols are shorthand: read them aloud and they become sentences.

01 Meaning

Notation is shorthand, like “tsp” for teaspoon. Each mark is a word or a hint about how to read the symbol next to it. A few turn up everywhere:

MarkRead aloudMeaning
x₃“x sub 3”a subscript picks the third entry
ŷ“y hat”a hat marks an estimate or prediction
x ∈ S“x is in S”element of a set
∀ i“for all i”for all: true for every one
𝐱“bold x”a vector: a list of numbers
ℝ“the reals”the real numbers

02 Picture

The list x has five entries (in print, a list like this is often set in bold). The small number below each is a subscript: a position label that says which entry. Move the slider to point at a different box.

03 Real life

  • Chess notation: “Nf3” packs a piece and a square into three characters.
  • Sheet music: dots on a staff stand for pitch and length.
  • Recipe shorthand: “2 tbsp”, “350°F”.

Recognition rule. A symbol you don't know: check what is attached to it (a subscript, a hat, bold type), read the whole line aloud, then look the symbol up.

Spot it. How do you read “x₂ ∈ ℝ”?

04 Worked example

Predict, then reveal

Count along the list: the subscript is the position, starting at 1.

05 Why and when

Notation packs a long sentence into a line so you can see the structure at a glance. Without it, a formula about a thousand numbers would be a thousand words.

It only helps if you can read it, so when stuck, translate the line into words one mark at a time.

06 How to arrive at it

◆ Convention

Every mark here is a Convention. Books differ: some draw vectors with an arrow instead of bold, some number entries from 0. Check the notation page of whatever you are reading.

07 In an LLM

The link is conceptual: papers about language models are written in this notation, with data as xᵢ, predictions as ŷ and vectors in bold, so reading it is a prerequisite for reading them. Nothing about a model changes with the notation.

Teach it

Everyday analogy

Street numbers: “12” on a road picks one house out of many. Where it breaks down: house numbers are not always consecutive, subscripts usually are.

Common misconception

“A subscript means multiply.” It is a label for which item, nothing more.

Further reading: the notation table at the start of MML.
