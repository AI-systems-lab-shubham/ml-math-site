Σ notation

Σ is a loop that adds: run the counter, work out each term, total them.

01 Meaning

Think of a shop receipt. To get the total you go down the lines and add each price. A sum written with the Greek capital letter Σ (sigma) is that same instruction, packed small.

Under the Σ sits a counter, called the index, and where it starts. Above it sits where the counter stops. To the right is the recipe for each term. For example:

Σ i² = 1² + 2² + 3² + 4²

It reads “add up i squared, for i from 1 to 4”.

02 Picture

Each bar is one term. Filled bars are inside the range and are added; outlined bars are skipped. Change where the counter starts and stops, or the recipe, and watch the expanded sum below.

Expand a sum

03 Real life

  • A receipt total: one term per line.
  • Steps walked in a week: one term per day.
  • A road trip's distance: one term per leg.
  • Rainfall for a month: one term per day.

Recognition rule. If you are adding up one number for each item in a list, you are doing a Σ.

Spot it. A bike ride has four legs of lengths d₁, d₂, d₃, d₄ kilometres. Which expression is the total distance?

04 Worked example

Predict, then reveal

Write the four terms for i = 1, 2, 3, 4, then add them.

05 Why and when

Without Σ you would write “a₁ + a₂ + … + a₁₀₀₀” and hope the reader fills in the dots correctly. Σ states the rule exactly and works for any length, including a length n that is not yet known.

Use it for adding a family of terms that follow one recipe. If the terms have no pattern, just list them. If a stop value is below the start, the sum is empty and equals 0.

06 How to arrive at it

◆ Convention

The idea, a loop that adds terms, is not optional: any long sum needs some way to say “continue the pattern”. The symbols are a Convention: Σ for “sum”, i as the usual counter, start below and stop above. A book could swap them, and some do write the range after the symbol. What matters is that the rule, the start and the stop are all stated.

07 In an LLM

Two Σ's carry most of the load. The dot product of a list of weights with a list of inputs is a sum of products: with w = (2, −1, 3) and x = (4, 5, 1), it is 2·4 + (−1)·5 + 3·1 = …. A neuron computes this, and attention scores in a transformer are dot products too (Module 2).

The second is the loss. The total surprise of a text is the sum of −log₂ p over its tokens. For three tokens with probabilities ½, ¼ and ⅛ that is 1 + 2 + 3 = … bits, or … bits per token on average. Dividing by the number of tokens is the idea behind the per-token training loss for next-token prediction.

Simplified. Real training losses are usually reported in nats (natural logs) and averaged over many tokens and many texts, but the structure is the same: a sum of a per-token term.

Explain it back

The start says where the counter begins and the stop says where it ends. The expression on the right is the recipe for one term, written with the counter. Run the counter from start to stop, work out each term, then add them all.

Teach it

60-second explanation

“Σ means ‘add these up’. Underneath is a counter and where it starts, on top is where it stops, and on the right is the recipe for each term. Run the counter, work out each term, add. It is just a for-loop that keeps a running total.”

Everyday analogy

Adding up a receipt. Where it breaks down: a receipt lines are arbitrary prices; a Σ's terms come from one rule.

Whiteboard sequence

  1. Write Σ with start 1 and stop 4.
  2. Write the recipe i² on the right.
  3. Expand into 1² + 2² + 3² + 4².
  4. Work out each term.
  5. Add.

Questions learners ask

Does the counter have to be i? No, j, k or t work; it is just a label.

Can the start be 0? Yes, and in computing it often is.

What if the stop is smaller than the start? The sum is empty and equals 0.

Common misconception

“Σ i² is the same as (Σ i)².” It is not: 1 + 4 + 9 + 16 is not 10 squared.
