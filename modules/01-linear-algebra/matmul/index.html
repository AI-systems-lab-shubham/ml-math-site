Matrix-vector and matrix-matrix multiplication

Each entry of a product is a row dotted with a column. Chaining two machines is multiplying their matrices.

01 Meaning

In the last card, each output entry was “a row paired with the input, multiplied entry by entry, then added”. That is the dot product from the Σ card, and the whole recipe is the matrix-vector product: one dot product per row.

Back to x + y = 4. The one-row matrix [1 1] times the vector (x, y) is the single number x + y. For (x, y) = (1, 3) the product is …, so that pair solves the equation. Each equation is one row of weights; stack two equations and you have a two-row matrix. See Equations in Module 0.

Matrix multiplication chains two machines. Feed a vector through B, then through A. The single machine that does both in one go is AB, with vectors written as columns, as in most machine-learning texts. Its entry in row i, column j is the dot product of row i of A with column j of B. Each column of AB is A applied to the matching column of B.

For the dot products to exist, A must have as many columns as B has rows. Written with shapes, an m×n times an n×p gives m×p.

(AB)ᵢⱼ = Σₖ AᵢₖBₖⱼ

02 Picture

Pick an example, then step through the output entries. The outlined row of the left matrix and the outlined column of the right matrix pair up to fill one cell of the answer (burgundy). Try the two swap examples: the same two matrices in the opposite order give different answers. Try “shapes that do not fit” too.

Multiply, one output entry at a time

03 Real life

  • Total cost of a menu: ingredients per dish, then cost per ingredient, combined into cost per dish.
  • Chained unit conversions: kilometres to miles, then miles to something else, folded into one conversion.
  • Two photo filters applied in turn: one combined filter does the same job.
  • Population counts moving between cities each year: two years of moves, folded into one.

Recognition rule. If one weighted-total step feeds another, the two together are one matrix product, and the inner sizes must match.

Spot it. Table A gives kilograms of 3 ingredients in each of 2 dishes (3×2). Table B gives the price per kilogram of each ingredient in 2 shops (2×3). Which product gives the cost of each dish in each shop?

04 Worked example

Predict, then reveal

Row 2 of A is (4, 5, 6). Column 1 of B is (7, 9, 11). Pair them up and add.

05 Why and when

It lets you fold many chained steps into one machine, and it lets a computer do all the dot products in one regular pattern. Without the rule, “apply B then A” would need to be spelled out every time.

When it does not fit. The inner sizes must match. The order matters: AB and BA are usually different, and one can exist without the other. Multiplying matching entries of two same-shaped tables is a different operation, so do not confuse the two.

Cost. An m×n times n×p product takes m·n·p multiplications; the widget shows the count for each example.

06 How to arrive at it

✓ Derived

  1. The requirement. The product AB must be the machine that does B first, then A: (AB)v = A(Bv) for every v.
  2. Obvious candidate. Multiply matching entries of A and B.
  3. Why it fails. For P = [[1, 2], [3, 4]] and Q = [[0, 1], [1, 1]], applying Q then P to (1, 0) gives …. The matching-entries table sends (1, 0) to …. They disagree.
  4. Why the row-by-column rule remains. Write out A(Bv). Each entry of the result collects, from each column of B, a weighted sum of A's entries: a row of A paired with a column of B. That rule is forced by the requirement.

Before you continue, predict: for P and Q above, is PQ the same as QP?

I have made my prediction: show the reasoning

PQ is . Swapping the order, as the swap example in the widget shows, changes the answer in general: doing B then A is a different journey from doing A then B. Order matters because the first machine's output is the second machine's input.

07 In an LLM

  • Projections and feed-forward layers are matrix products. The query, key and value projections and the two feed-forward matrices all work by matrix multiplication (Vaswani et al., 2017, §3.2 and §3.3). A … matrix times one …-entry vector takes … multiplications.
  • A large share of the arithmetic. Much of a transformer's computation is matrix multiplication, including these layers and the attention-score step. We do not give a percentage here, since it depends on the model size and the sequence length.
  • Hardware. A GPU can do many independent multiplications and additions at once, which suits the many independent dot products inside a matrix product. This is general public knowledge about why such chips are used, not a statement about any company's systems.

Simplified. Real systems process many tokens at once, so the right-hand matrix has many columns instead of one. The attention scores multiply two matrices built from the same inputs (Module 2 and Module 10 build this up).

Explain it back

Each output entry pairs a row of the left matrix with a column of the right one, and pairing needs equal lengths, so the left's column count must equal the right's row count. The order matters because the product means “do the right one first, then the left one”, and the output of the first step is the input of the second.

Teach it

60-second explanation

“Multiplying a matrix by a vector means: take each row, pair it with the vector, multiply matching numbers and add. Multiplying two matrices means chaining two machines into one. To fill each cell of the answer, take a row from the left matrix and a column from the right matrix, pair them off and add. The sizes must fit: columns on the left equal rows on the right. And the order matters, because the right machine runs first.”

Everyday analogy

Two currency converters in a row: euros to pounds, then pounds to dollars. One combined converter does both. Where it breaks down: currency rates are single numbers, and single numbers can be multiplied in either order; matrices cannot.

Whiteboard sequence

  1. Draw A and B side by side and write their shapes under them.
  2. Check the inner sizes match and circle them.
  3. Draw the empty answer table of the outer sizes.
  4. Pick a cell, outline its row in A and column in B.
  5. Pair, multiply, add, and write the number in the cell.

Questions learners ask

Why is it not just entry times entry? That would not chain machines. Row-by-column is what makes “B then A” correct.

Is AB always the same as BA? No. Often one does not even exist.

Is a vector a matrix? It can be treated as a matrix with one column, which is why matrix-vector is a special case.

Common misconception

“Matrix multiplication multiplies matching entries.” That is a different operation. Matrix multiplication pairs a row with a column and adds.
