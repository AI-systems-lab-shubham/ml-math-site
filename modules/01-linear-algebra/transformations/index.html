Linear transformations: rotate, scale, shear, project

What a matrix does to the whole plane at once: turn it, stretch it, lean it over, or squash it flat.

01 Meaning

Draw a grid on a rubber sheet, pin the origin, and then move the sheet. If you only turn it, stretch it evenly along its axes, lean it sideways, or squash it onto a line, then straight lines stay straight, the grid squares stay equally spaced parallelograms, and the origin stays put. A rule that moves the plane like that is a linear transformation.

On the Matrices card we saw that a matrix sends the unit arrows (1, 0) and (0, 1) to its two columns, and that fixes where every other point goes. So every linear transformation of the plane is a 2×2 matrix, and the matrix is just the record of where the two unit arrows land. Four standard ones:

  • A rotation turns the plane by an angle θ, counter-clockwise on the page, with vectors written as columns.
  • Scaling stretches the x-direction by sx and the y-direction by sy.
  • A shear leans the square over: the top slides sideways by k for each unit of height.
  • A projection onto the x-axis drops every point straight down onto the x-axis.

03 Real life

  • Rotating a photo in an editor: every pixel position is turned about the centre.
  • Zooming a map along one direction only: a stretch in x or y.
  • Italic type: upright letters leaned over, a shear.
  • A shadow of a building at noon on the ground: a flattening onto a surface, like a projection.

Recognition rule. If a motion keeps straight lines straight and leaves the origin where it is, it is a linear transformation, and a matrix describes it. If it squashes the plane, the matrix has no inverse.

Spot it. A drawing app slides every point to the right by 2 units, whatever its position. Is that a linear transformation of the plane?

05 Why and when

Thinking of a matrix as a motion of space gives it a shape you can see: is it a turn, a stretch, a lean, a squash, or a mix? Chaining motions is multiplying matrices, and undoing a motion is taking the inverse, so the earlier cards come together here.

Invertible or not. A rotation loses no information, so it can be undone by turning back. A projection squashes the plane onto a line, so many points share one image: its rank is 1 and it has no inverse. Scaling by 0 does the same.

When it does not fit. Motions that move the origin (a slide), bend lines (a curve), or have limits (a maximum) are not linear. A translation can be written with an extra number added on, which is a different, slightly larger idea.

06 How to arrive at it

✓ Derived

  1. The requirement. A rotation by θ must keep lengths and angles, so the arrow (1, 0) lands at length 1, at angle θ.
  2. Where (1, 0) lands. At angle θ on the unit circle: (cos θ, sin θ). That is column 1.
  3. Where (0, 1) lands. It started 90° counter-clockwise from (1, 0), so it lands 90° counter-clockwise from (cos θ, sin θ), which is (−sin θ, cos θ). That is column 2.
  4. The matrix. Columns (cos θ, sin θ) and (−sin θ, cos θ) give rows (cos θ, −sin θ) and (sin θ, cos θ). The same idea gives the other three: write down where the unit arrows land, and the columns follow. The letters sx, sy, k and θ are names; the matrices are not a convention, but a counter-clockwise positive angle is.

Before you continue, predict: where does a 90° counter-clockwise rotation send (1, 0)? And (0, 1)?

I have made my prediction: show the reasoning

(1, 0) turns a quarter-turn to point straight up: (0, 1). (0, 1) turns a quarter-turn to point left: (−1, 0). Those are the two columns of the 90° matrix, as the first two steps of the worked example show.

07 In an LLM

  • Every linear layer is a linear map, plus a shift. The projections and feed-forward matrices of the original Transformer are matrices, so (ignoring biases) each one is a linear transformation, and it moves a whole space of vectors at once (Vaswani et al., 2017, §3.2 and §3.3). With a bias added it is a linear map followed by a slide, which is no longer linear in the strict sense.
  • Why there is a nonlinearity in between. Two linear maps with nothing between them collapse into one matrix: applying in a single step to (3, −1) gives …, the same as applying the two in turn (…). The feed-forward layer of the original Transformer therefore puts a ReLU between its two matrices (Vaswani et al., 2017, §3.3).

Simplified. The example matrices are small made-up numbers, not weights from a model. Real layers also add biases and use many heads or very wide matrices.

Explain it back

Every point is some amount of (1, 0) plus some amount of (0, 1), and a linear transformation keeps scaling and adding, so the image is the same amounts of the two landing points. A projection squashes the plane onto a line, so different points share one image, and from the image alone you cannot tell which point it came from.

Teach it

60-second explanation

“A matrix moves the whole plane. Look at where the two unit arrows land: those landing places are the columns. A rotation turns, a scaling stretches, a shear leans, a projection squashes onto a line. Rotations, scalings with no zero and shears can be undone. A projection cannot, because many points end up in the same place.”

Everyday analogy

A rubber sheet with a grid drawn on it: pin the middle, then turn, stretch, lean or flatten it. Where it breaks down: a real sheet can be folded or torn; a linear transformation cannot fold, only squash flat.

Whiteboard sequence

  1. Draw the unit square with arrows (1, 0) and (0, 1).
  2. Choose a motion and sketch where each arrow lands.
  3. Write the two landing points as columns.
  4. Sketch the image of the square.
  5. Ask: can it be undone? Check whether the image is flat.

Questions learners ask

Does the order of two transformations matter? Yes: the matrices are multiplied, and a product usually changes when its factors are swapped.

Why counter-clockwise? It is the standard convention for positive angles; the opposite direction is just a negative angle.

What does a negative determinant mean? Here: the square is flipped over like a mirror image.

Common misconception

“A transformation moves each point independently.” A linear transformation moves all points by one consistent rule: the landing places of the two unit arrows decide everything.
