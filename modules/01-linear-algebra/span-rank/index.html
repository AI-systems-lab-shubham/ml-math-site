Span, linear independence, basis and rank

Which points a set of arrows can reach, whether any arrow is redundant, and how many genuinely different directions a matrix has.

01 Meaning

Picture a robot on a flat floor with two controls. One control drives it along one direction, the other along a second direction, and you can use each control by any amount, forwards or backwards. The set of floor points it can reach is the span of those two directions: everything you can make as a linear combination of them.

If the two directions are really different, the robot reaches the whole floor. If they are the same line (one control is just a multiple of the other), it reaches only that line: one control is redundant. Vectors where none is redundant are linearly independent. Independent vectors whose span is the whole space are a basis: no redundancy, and nothing out of reach. In the plane, two independent arrows are a basis.

Count the directions in a matrix by looking at its columns. The rank is the number of independent columns. A 2×2 matrix has rank 2, 1 or 0.

span(v₁, v₂) = { c₁v₁ + c₂v₂ }

02 Picture

The tinted plane is the span. Change v₁ and v₂, or press “Same line” to make them dependent: the tint shrinks to a line. The burgundy dot w is a target. When it can be reached, the dashed arrows show a recipe, and the text gives the amounts of v₁ and v₂.

03 Real life

  • Mixing paint from red, yellow and blue: the colours you can mix are the span of the three, and a fourth tin that is just a mix of the first three adds nothing.
  • A crane that moves along a rail and hoists up and down: two independent controls reach every point in its vertical plane.
  • A spreadsheet with a total column that always equals the sum of two other columns: that column is redundant, and the table has fewer independent columns than it looks.
  • A set of survey questions where two ask almost the same thing: fewer genuine directions than questions.

Recognition rule. If one item can be built from the others, it is redundant (dependent). Count what is left after removing redundancy: that count is the rank, or the size of a basis.

Spot it. A table has three columns: price, tax and total, where total is always price plus tax. How many independent columns does it have?

05 Why and when

These ideas answer “can I reach it?” and “am I wasting effort?”. In the last card, Ax = b has a solution exactly when b is in the span of the columns of A. A basis gives every point exactly one recipe, so coordinates mean something. Rank measures how much a matrix really does.

When it does not fit. These are about linear recipes only (scaling and adding). They say nothing about curves or limits on amounts.

06 How to arrive at it

● Motivated

  1. The question. Which targets can I reach by scaling and adding my vectors?
  2. The first answer. Define the span as exactly that set.
  3. The next question. Is any vector wasted? A vector is wasted if it is already in the span of the others. Call a set with no wasted vectors independent.
  4. The pair together. A set that reaches everything with nothing wasted is a basis. Counting the independent columns of a matrix is the rank.

These are good definitions rather than forced ones: other ways to phrase them exist, but these keep “reach” and “redundancy” separate.

Before you continue, predict: can three vectors in the plane be independent?

I have made my prediction: show the reasoning

No. Two independent arrows already reach every point of the plane, so a third arrow is always inside their span. A plane has room for only two independent directions.

07 In an LLM

  • A low-rank update. LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) adjusts a large weight matrix by adding the product of two thin matrices, a d×r one times an r×k one, with r much smaller than d and k. That product has rank at most r. For a …×… matrix (… entries) and an illustrative r = 8, the two thin matrices hold … numbers in all.
  • A small example of the rank bound. A 4×1 times a 1×3 matrix is 4×3 with … entries but rank …, and can be stored with … numbers.

Simplified. This is only the rank and counting idea, not how LoRA is trained or which layers it is applied to. The value r = 8 is chosen for illustration; the paper studies several values of r.

Teach it

Everyday analogy

A music mixer with sliders: the sounds you can make are the span of the channels. If two channels carry the same signal, one slider is redundant. Where it breaks down: real sliders have limits; a span allows any amount, positive or negative.

Common misconception

“More vectors always reach more.” Only independent ones do. A vector already in the span adds nothing, and the rank does not grow.
