Eigendecomposition

Change to the eigenvector directions, stretch each, change back: and what that makes easy.

01 Meaning

Suppose a matrix has two independent eigenvectors. Then you can describe any arrow in terms of them, and in those directions the matrix does nothing more than multiply by a number. So to apply the matrix: switch to the eigenvector directions, stretch each by its eigenvalue, then switch back. Written as a product, that is the eigendecomposition A = P D P−1.

P has the eigenvectors as its columns, so P−1 switches into their directions. D is a diagonal matrix holding the eigenvalues, which stretches each direction on its own. P then switches back. It only works when there are enough independent eigenvectors to fill the columns of P; the shear from the last card does not have them.

A = P D P⁻¹

02 Picture

This matrix is symmetric, which makes the picture simple: its eigenvectors are along the two diagonals, at right angles. The faint circle is the unit circle; the filled shape is what An makes of it. Raise n and see the longer axis grow with the bigger eigenvalue and the shorter one shrink with the smaller one.

Powers of a symmetric matrix

03 Real life

  • A table of year-to-year rates between a few groups, such as the young, adult and old members of a population. Applying one year’s table n years in a row is the matrix power An.
  • A savings account where interest is added each year: the amount after n years is the starting amount times a growth factor raised to the power n. Eigendecomposition does the same for a whole table of growth factors at once.
  • A change of units: to work in kilometres, compute, and convert back, which is the same switch-in, act, switch-out pattern as P, D, P−1.

Recognition rule. If a task has the shape “change to better coordinates, do something simple, change back”, think of P D P−1.

04 Worked example

05 Why and when

Powers become cheap: An = P Dn P−1, because the P−1P pairs in the middle cancel and a diagonal matrix is raised to a power entry by entry. After many steps the largest eigenvalue dominates, so an arrow that is repeatedly multiplied by A turns towards the matching eigenvector.

A symmetric matrix always works. A symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues, and its eigenvectors can be chosen at right angles (see the Orthogonality card in Module 2), so P can be an orthogonal matrix Q whose inverse is just its transpose: A = Q Λ QT.

When it does not fit. A matrix with too few independent eigenvectors, like the shear, cannot be written as P D P−1, and neither can a rotation, which has no real eigenvalues. A matrix that is not square has no eigenvectors.

06 How to arrive at it

✓ Derived

  1. Start from the eigen-equations. A v₁ = λ₁v₁ and A v₂ = λ₂v₂.
  2. Stack them side by side. A [v₁ v₂] = [λ₁v₁ λ₂v₂]. The right side is [v₁ v₂] times the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues. In symbols, A P = P D.
  3. Undo P. If P has an inverse, which needs the eigenvectors to be independent, multiply on the right by P−1: A = P D P−1.
  4. If P has no inverse, there are too few independent eigenvectors and the step fails: that is exactly the defective case.

07 In an LLM

  • Nothing specific to claim. One caution: An applies the same matrix n times, whereas the layers of a transformer each have their own learned weights (Vaswani et al., 2017), so powers of a single matrix do not describe how a transformer’s depth works.

Teach it

Everyday analogy

Cooking in your friend’s kitchen, with your friend’s units: convert the recipe, cook, convert back. Where it breaks down: the conversion here is a matrix, and if it has no inverse the trick fails.

Common misconception

“Every square matrix can be written as P D P⁻¹.” No: the shear has only one eigenvector direction, and a rotation has no real eigenvectors.
