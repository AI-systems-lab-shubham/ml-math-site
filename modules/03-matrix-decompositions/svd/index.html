Singular value decomposition

Every matrix, however awkward, is rotate, stretch, rotate.

01 Meaning

Press a round lump of dough through any matrix and what comes out is an ellipse (or, if the matrix flattens it, a line). That is true even when the matrix has no eigenvectors, and even when it is not square. The ellipse has a longest and a shortest direction, at right angles to each other, and the matrix can be seen as three simple moves: turn or flip the input so that the right two directions line up with the axes, stretch along the axes, then turn or flip the result into place.

That is the singular value decomposition, A = U Σ VT. VT and U are orthogonal matrices: they turn or flip without stretching. Σ (capital sigma) is a diagonal matrix holding the singular values σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ … ≥ 0, which are the semi-axes of the ellipse. The columns of V and of U are the singular vectors, with A v = σ u.

A = U Σ V^T

02 Picture

The dashed circle is the unit circle. Step through the three moves: V^T turns or flips the circle (it stays a circle), Σ stretches it into an ellipse along the axes, and U turns or flips the ellipse into its final place, which is exactly what A does. The ochre and navy arrows are the two right singular vectors carried through each stage; at the end they are σ₁u₁ and σ₂u₂. The widget shows 2×2 matrices; for an m×n matrix the circle becomes an ellipsoid in a space of a different size.

Rotate, stretch, rotate

03 Real life

  • A round rubber seal squeezed between two fingers into an oval: the oval’s long and short axes are the stretch directions, and their lengths are the singular values.
  • A circular plate seen at a slant: the circle appears as an ellipse whose long axis is the plate’s width and whose short axis is that width times the cosine of the tilt.
  • A square of cloth pulled corner to corner and sheared: the diagonal it stretches most is σ₁, and the one it stretches least is σ₂.

Recognition rule. If you ask “what is the biggest stretch this matrix gives, along which direction, and what is the smallest?”, it is the SVD.

04 Worked example

Check by hand: the shear has det = 1, and σ₁σ₂ = …, as it must, since the product of the singular values is |det A|. Its entries squared add to …, which equals σ₁² + σ₂².

05 Why and when

The SVD works for every real matrix, which is its strength. It gives the largest stretch (σ₁, also called the spectral norm), the rank (the number of non-zero singular values), and the sum of squared entries (σ₁² + σ₂² + …). The singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues of ATA, which are never negative.

Rank, with a tolerance. On a computer a singular value that should be 0 comes out as a tiny number. The numerical rank counts the singular values above max(m, n) × ε × σ₁, where ε = … is the rounding size of double-precision numbers. For the shear this tolerance is …, far below σ₂.

When it does not fit, and what is not unique. U and V are not unique: you can flip the sign of a matching column of each, and if two singular values are equal any rotation inside their plane works. U and V may include flips, not only rotations (determinant −1). The singular values themselves are unique.

06 How to arrive at it

◆ Motivated

  1. The wish. Find perpendicular input directions that A sends to perpendicular output directions, so that A acts as a pure stretch between them.
  2. A good candidate. ATA is symmetric, so by the last card it has real eigenvalues and eigenvectors at right angles, v₁ and v₂. We pick it because it works, not because anything forced it: that is why this is Motivated.
  3. The stretch. The length of A vi squared is viTATA vi = λi, so A vi has length √λi = σi, never negative.
  4. The directions out. (A vi)·(A vj) = viTATA vj = λj vi·vj = 0 for i ≠ j, so the outputs are at right angles too. Divide by σi to get unit vectors ui.

07 In an LLM

  • Nothing specific to claim. The SVD is a tool for understanding and approximating matrices. This card makes no claim about where it appears inside a language model; the next card gives one documented use of a related idea.

Simplified. The widget shows 2×2 matrices only; the SVD of a larger or non-square matrix has the same form but cannot be drawn on a page.

Teach it

60-second explanation

“Any matrix turns a circle into an ellipse. The SVD says exactly how: turn or flip so the ellipse’s axes line up, stretch by the singular values along the axes, then turn or flip again. The singular values are the ellipse’s semi-axes, the biggest is the largest stretch, and the number of non-zero ones is the rank.”

Everyday analogy

Squashing a round pizza base into an oval by pulling along two perpendicular lines. Where it breaks down: the matrix can also flip the base over and can squash it to a line, which dough does not do.

Whiteboard sequence

  1. Draw the unit circle and a matrix’s ellipse beside it.
  2. Draw the ellipse’s two axes and mark their lengths σ₁ and σ₂.
  3. Write A = U Σ V^T under it.
  4. Label V^T ‘turn’, Σ ‘stretch’, U ‘turn’.
  5. Mark that σ₂ = 0 flattens the ellipse to a line.

Questions learners ask

Does it work for non-square matrices? Yes. U and V then have different sizes, and the circle becomes an ellipsoid in the output space.

How is it different from eigendecomposition? It always exists, uses two different orthogonal matrices, and has no negative or complex numbers; eigendecomposition needs enough eigenvectors.

Are U and V unique? No: signs can be flipped in matching pairs, and repeated singular values leave a free rotation.

Common misconception

“The singular values are the eigenvalues.” They are the square roots of the eigenvalues of A^T A. For a symmetric matrix they are the absolute values of its eigenvalues, but for a general matrix the two lists differ.
