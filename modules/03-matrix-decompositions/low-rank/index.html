Low-rank approximation

Keep the biggest layers of a matrix, drop the rest, and know exactly how much error that costs.

01 Meaning

The SVD writes a matrix as a sum of simple layers: A = σ₁u₁v₁T + σ₂u₂v₂T + …, ordered from the biggest stretch to the smallest. Each layer is a column times a row, so it has rank 1. If the later layers are tiny, leaving them out changes the matrix very little. Keeping only the first k layers gives a low-rank approximation Ak, a matrix of rank at most k.

The Eckart–Young theorem says this is the best you can do: no other matrix of rank k is closer to A. The error is exactly the layers you dropped.

A_k = σ₁u₁v₁^T + … + σₖuₖvₖ^T; squared error = σₖ₊₁² + σₖ₊₂² + …

03 Real life

  • A multiplication table: the entry in row i and column j is i × j, a column times a row, so the whole table has rank …. One layer rebuilds it exactly.
  • A photograph with a big smooth sky: most of its columns look alike, so a few layers capture most of it and the many small ones are fine detail.
  • A table of how much each person likes each film: if tastes come in a few broad kinds, a few layers describe the whole table.

Recognition rule. If a table’s columns look like mixtures of a few patterns, a low-rank approximation will describe it with far fewer numbers.

05 Why and when

Storing a rank-k approximation of an m×n matrix takes k columns of U, k of V and k singular values: k(m + n + 1) numbers, against mn for the matrix itself. For 512×512 and k = 8 that is … against ….

When it does not fit. The saving needs k(m + n + 1) to be less than mn. For the 16×16 pattern each layer costs … numbers against … for the picture, so ranks above … store more than the picture (rank 8 stores …). Low rank also only helps when the singular values fall quickly: if they are all about equal, dropping any of them loses a lot.

06 How to arrive at it

✓ Derived

  1. The requirement. Among all matrices of rank at most k, find the one closest to A, measuring distance by the sum of squared entry differences (squared Frobenius norm).
  2. Layers do not overlap. The layers σiuiviT are orthogonal to each other, so the squared size of a sum of layers is the sum of the squared sizes: σ₁² + σ₂² + … This is why the squared error of dropping layers is the sum of their squared singular values.
  3. The theorem. Eckart and Young showed that keeping the first k layers is the best possible: no rank-k matrix has a smaller error, and the spectral error is σk+1. We do not reproduce the proof here (it is in MML Ch 4.6).
  4. What the tests do. They try thousands of random rank-k matrices and find none closer. That is a check, not a proof.

07 In an LLM

  • LoRA (low-rank adaptation). To fine-tune a large model cheaply, Hu et al. (2021, arXiv:2106.09685) freeze the pre-trained weights and inject trainable rank-decomposition matrices: the weight matrix W₀ becomes W₀ + ΔW = W₀ + BA, where B is d×r and A is r×k with r small. Here, for a 512×512 matrix and r = …, B is … and A is ….
  • The hypothesis. The paper’s hypothesis is that the weight updates made during fine-tuning have a low intrinsic rank, which is why a low-rank update can be enough. In its experiments, a rank of one or two can suffice for GPT-3 175B, whose full rank is 12,288. The trainable parameters can be as small as 0.01% of those of GPT-3 175B, and the update can be merged into the frozen weights, so it adds no inference latency.
  • How it differs from truncated SVD. LoRA is related to low-rank approximation, but it does not compute an SVD: it learns the two factors by training. A truncated SVD stores k(m + n + 1) = … numbers for 512×512 at k = 8; LoRA stores 2×512×8 = …, with no singular values (… fewer).

Simplified. The paper reports results for particular models, ranks and tasks; these numbers are not a general rule. This card does not describe how any company’s models are fine-tuned beyond what that paper states.

Teach it

60-second explanation

“Break a matrix into layers with the SVD, biggest first. Keep the first k and you have the best rank-k copy there can be. The squared error is the sum of the squares of the layers you dropped, and it costs k times (m plus n plus one) numbers instead of m times n. LoRA uses the same shape of idea, a thin product BA, but learns the two thin matrices instead of computing an SVD.”

Everyday analogy

A compressed photo: keep the broad shapes and drop fine detail. Where it breaks down: the layers here are ordered by a precise size, and the loss is exactly the dropped layers, which is not true of every compression method.

Whiteboard sequence

  1. Write A as a sum of three layers with σ₁ > σ₂ > σ₃.
  2. Cross out the last layer to get A₂.
  3. Write error² = σ₃².
  4. Draw A (m×n) as a tall thin box times a wide thin box.
  5. Count the numbers in the thin boxes against m×n.

Questions learners ask

Is the approximation exact at full rank? Yes. With k equal to the number of singular values, nothing is dropped and the error is 0.

Why does a rank-8 update of 512×512 store so little? Because it is stored as two thin matrices, 512×8 and 8×512, not as 262,144 separate entries.

Does LoRA compute an SVD? No. It trains the two factors directly; low-rank approximation is the related idea.

Common misconception

“LoRA is a truncated SVD of the weights.” It is not: LoRA learns the low-rank update by training, and it never computes an SVD of the weights.
