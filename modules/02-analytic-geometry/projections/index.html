Projections

Split an arrow into the part that lies along another arrow and a leftover at right angles to it.

01 Meaning

The projection of a onto b is the shadow of a on the line through b, drawn as an arrow. It is some number of copies of b: the same direction as b, or the opposite one if the dot product is negative. What is left of a once you take that shadow away is the leftover, and it meets b at a right angle, so it is orthogonal to b.

To find the number of copies, divide the dot product by b·b. For a = (3, 1) and b = (1, 1) the projection is …, and the leftover a minus that is …. Projecting onto the x-axis direction (1, 0), as on the Linear transformations card, sends (3, 2) to ….

proj_b a = ((a·b) / (b·b)) b

02 Picture

The ochre arrow is the projection of a onto b. The dashed teal arrow is the leftover, and the small square marks the right angle where it meets b’s line. Change a and b. Set b to (0, 0) to see why there is nothing to project onto.

03 Real life

  • The shadow of a pole on a straight road: its length along the road is the projection.
  • Wind on a cyclist: only the part of the wind along the road helps or hinders; the sideways part does not.
  • A satnav “snapping” your position to the nearest point of a straight road: the nearest point is the projection of your position onto the road.
  • A ladder against a wall: its horizontal reach is the projection of the ladder onto the ground.

Recognition rule. If you ask “how much of this arrow lies along that direction?” and want the answer as an arrow, it is a projection.

Spot it. The wind blows with velocity (3, 1) and you cycle along the heading (1, 1). Which part of the wind helps or hinders you?

04 Worked example

Predict, then reveal

Find a·b and b·b first. The coefficient is their quotient. Multiply (0, 3) by it.

05 Why and when

Projection answers “what is the closest point on this line to a?”: the projection, because the leftover is as short as it can be when it is at right angles. That is the idea behind fitting a line to data.

When it does not fit. Projecting onto the zero vector is undefined: it has no direction, so there is no line to project onto, and the code must check. Projection also throws information away: many arrows share one projection, so it cannot be undone, as the Linear transformations card showed for the x-axis.

06 How to arrive at it

✓ Derived

  1. The requirement. Write a as some number k of copies of b, plus a leftover r that is orthogonal to b: a = k b + r with r·b = 0.
  2. Take the dot product with b. a·b = k (b·b) + r·b = k (b·b), because r·b = 0.
  3. Solve. k = (a·b) / (b·b), so the projection is k b. For a = (3, 1) and b = (1, 1): k = ….
  4. Check. The leftover a − k b is orthogonal to b by construction, and the tests confirm it on hundreds of random pairs.

Before you continue, predict: if a already lies along b, what is the leftover?

I have made my prediction: show the reasoning

It is the zero vector. All of a is already along b, so the projection is a itself and nothing is left over.

07 In an LLM

  • The word “projection” has a second meaning here. Transformer papers use “linear projections” for learned linear maps: in the original Transformer, queries, keys and values are made by multiplying by learned matrices (Vaswani et al., 2017, §3.2.2). Those are not orthogonal projections. An orthogonal projection onto a line is a square operation you can repeat without changing the result; a learned projection from … entries down to … has a … shape and cannot even be applied twice.

Simplified. This only separates two uses of one word. It makes no claim about what a trained projection matrix has learned.

Explain it back

The projection is the number k of copies of b that makes the leftover have no part along b. Taking the dot product of a = k b + r with b shows that the only choice that makes r·b = 0 is k = (a·b)/(b·b). With that k, the leftover is orthogonal to b by construction.

Teach it

60-second explanation

“The projection of a onto b is the shadow of a on b’s line. To find it, divide a·b by b·b to see how many copies of b you need, then multiply b by that. What is left, a minus the shadow, meets b at a right angle.”

Everyday analogy

A flagpole’s shadow on flat ground at noon. Where it breaks down: a real shadow cannot point backwards, but a projection onto b points opposite to b when a leans against it.

Whiteboard sequence

  1. Draw b and a from one point.
  2. Drop a perpendicular from a’s tip to b’s line.
  3. Mark the foot: that is the projection.
  4. Write k = (a·b)/(b·b) and projection = k b.
  5. Mark the right angle and call the dashed segment the leftover.

Questions learners ask

Is the projection longer than a? Never. It is at most as long as a.

What if b is the zero vector? There is no line, so the projection is undefined.

Does it matter how long b is? No: only b’s direction counts; doubling b halves k but leaves k b unchanged.

Common misconception

“Projecting onto b means taking the dot product.” The dot product is one number; the projection is an arrow, that number divided by b·b and multiplied by b.
