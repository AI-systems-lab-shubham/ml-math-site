Orthogonality

Two arrows are orthogonal when their dot product is zero: a right angle, extended to any number of entries.

01 Meaning

North and east are at right angles: moving east does nothing to how far north you are. Two vectors with that property are orthogonal. On the Dot product card we saw that at 90° the cosine is 0, so the dot product is 0. Turn that around and make it the definition: a and b are orthogonal when a·b = 0. The definition works for any number of entries, where “a right angle” is hard to draw, and it covers the zero vector: its dot product with anything is 0, so the zero vector counts as orthogonal to every vector.

a·b = 0

03 Real life

  • Two walls meeting at a corner of a room: their directions are at right angles.
  • The x-axis and the y-axis of a graph.
  • A wind blowing due north on a cart rolling due east: it does no work on the cart’s motion (see the Dot product card).

Recognition rule. If the dot product is exactly 0, the arrows are orthogonal.

Spot it. A cart rolls due east and the wind blows exactly due north. Ignoring friction, does the wind push the cart faster or slower along its path?

04 Worked example

Predict, then reveal

Write out the dot product 2×k + 1×4 and set it equal to 0.

05 Why and when

Orthogonal directions do not interfere, so each can be handled separately. That is the idea behind perpendicular axes on a graph: moving along one never changes your position along the other.

When it does not fit. Real computed numbers carry rounding error, so code usually tests that the dot product is very small compared with the lengths rather than exactly 0.

06 How to arrive at it

◆ Convention

  1. The idea. Right angles, where the cosine is 0.
  2. The choice. We could say “the angle is 90°”. That fails for the zero vector and is awkward with many entries, so we define the property by a·b = 0 instead.
  3. The consequence. The zero vector becomes orthogonal to everything. That is a convention that keeps statements simple, not something forced by a picture.

Before you continue, predict: is the zero vector orthogonal to itself?

I have made my prediction: show the reasoning

Yes: 0·0 = 0, so the definition holds. The zero vector is orthogonal to every vector, including itself.

07 In an LLM

  • Nothing specific to claim. Orthogonality is a geometric property. This card makes no statement about language models.
Teach it

Everyday analogy

A compass: moving east never changes your north–south position. Where it breaks down: in many dimensions there are more than two perpendicular directions, which a flat compass cannot show.

Common misconception

“Orthogonal means unrelated.” It means the dot product is zero, a statement about directions. Two vectors can be orthogonal and still be tied together in other ways.
