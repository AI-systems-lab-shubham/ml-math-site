Norms: L1 and L2

Two honest ways to say how long an arrow is: as the crow flies, or as the taxi drives along the grid.

01 Meaning

How long is an arrow? Walk 3 steps across and 4 steps up. You could answer by pulling a string tight from start to finish (the straight line), or by counting the steps you actually walked along the grid lines (3 + 4). Both are fair answers to “how far?”, and each one is a norm: a rule that turns a vector into a single length. Write the norm of x as ‖x‖.

The straight-line length is the L2 norm. The two walked legs and the straight line make a right triangle, so Pythagoras gives the length: square each entry, add the squares, take the square root. For (3, 4) that is …. In three entries the same idea repeats: (1, 2, 2) has length ….

The grid-walk length is the L1 norm: ignore the signs and add the sizes of the entries. For (3, 4) it is …. A step backwards is still a step you walked, so it counts as positive.

Any fair length obeys three rules: it is never negative; it is zero only for the zero vector; and doubling an arrow doubles its length. The small number under the bars says which norm: ‖x‖₂ and ‖x‖₁.

‖x‖₂ = √(x₁² + … + xₙ²), ‖x‖₁ = |x₁| + … + |xₙ|

03 Real life

  • Flying versus driving: the straight-line distance between two cities is an L2 length, the street-grid distance in a city laid out in blocks is an L1 length.
  • Pacing out a room: the diagonal across the floor is L2, walking along two walls is L1.
  • Total error: add up how far each prediction missed its target, ignoring whether it was too high or too low. That is the L1 norm of the list of errors.
  • A cable running diagonally across a rectangle of ground: its length is the L2 norm of (width, depth).

Recognition rule. If the length comes from squaring, adding and taking a root, it is L2. If it comes from adding sizes with the signs dropped, it is L1.

Spot it. Delivery drivers in a city built on a grid of blocks travel from a shop to a house that is 3 blocks east and 4 blocks north. Which length is the distance they actually drive?

05 Why and when

Many problems need “how big is this?” for a whole list of numbers: how large are a model’s weights, how big was the error? A norm gives that single number.

Which one. L2 squares each entry, so one big entry dominates, and it connects to angles and the dot product. L1 treats every entry in proportion to its size, so it is less swayed by one large entry. Neither is “the” length: they answer slightly different questions.

When it does not fit. A norm says how big a vector is, not which way it points. Two very different arrows can have the same length.

06 How to arrive at it

● Motivated

  1. The requirement. A length should be zero only for the zero vector, never negative, and double when the arrow doubles.
  2. Candidate 1: the sum of the entries. It fails: (3, −3) would have length 0 although it is a long arrow. Dropping signs fixes that, which gives L1.
  3. Candidate 2: the straight line. If a step across and a step up make a right angle, Pythagoras says the straight line is √(across² + up²). That is L2, and it is the one that matches a ruler.
  4. What remains. Both candidates meet the rules. Which one to use is a modelling choice, so L1 and L2 are good fits among several (a third, the largest entry, also works), not a single forced answer.

Before you continue, predict: can the L1 norm of a vector ever be smaller than its L2 norm?

I have made my prediction: show the reasoning

No. The straight line is the shortest route between two points, and the grid walk is one route, so the L1 length is at least as long as the L2 length. They are equal only when the arrow lies along an axis.

07 In an LLM

  • L2 regularisation. A common way to discourage large weights is to add a penalty to the loss that is proportional to the squared L2 norm of the weights (L2 regularisation): the total loss is the original loss plus (λ/2)‖w‖₂², where λ sets how strong the penalty is.
  • Weight decay. Weight decay shrinks every weight directly at each step instead: w ← (1 − ηλ)w − η·(gradient), where η is the learning rate. For plain stochastic gradient descent the two are equivalent once the strength is rescaled by the learning rate. For example, with w = (2, −1), loss gradient (0.5, 0.5), η = 0.1 and λ = 0.01, one penalty step gives … and one decay step gives …: the same.
  • Where they differ. For adaptive optimisers such as Adam, which rescale each weight’s step, the penalty’s gradient is rescaled along with the loss gradient while decoupled weight decay is not, so the two are no longer the same (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019, “Decoupled Weight Decay Regularization”). AdamW is Adam with decoupled weight decay, and is a common choice for training Transformers.

Simplified. The example numbers are made up to show the algebra, not taken from a model. This card does not say which penalty or strength any particular model uses.

Explain it back

The L2 norm is the straight-line length from Pythagoras, √(9 + 16) = 5. The L1 norm adds the sizes of the entries, 3 + 4 = 7, which is how far you walk along the grid. L1 is never shorter than L2, and the two agree only when the arrow lies along an axis.

Teach it

60-second explanation

“How long is an arrow? You can pull a string tight, that is the L2 length and it comes from Pythagoras. Or you can walk along the grid lines like a taxi, that is the L1 length and you just add up the sizes. Both are norms. They are zero only for the zero vector, and they double when the arrow doubles.”

Everyday analogy

Crow versus taxi: the crow flies straight, the taxi follows the streets. Where it breaks down: a real city has some diagonal roads; the L1 norm assumes you can only move along the two axes.

Whiteboard sequence

  1. Draw an arrow from the origin to (3, 4).
  2. Draw the right triangle under it and label the legs 3 and 4.
  3. Write √(3² + 4²) = 5 for the straight line.
  4. Trace the two legs in a different colour and write 3 + 4 = 7.
  5. Mark every point at L1 distance 5 from the origin: a diamond. At L2 distance 5: a circle.

Questions learners ask

Why square the entries for L2? Pythagoras: lengths along two perpendicular directions combine as squares.

Is the L1 norm ever smaller than L2? Never. The straight line is the shortest route.

Can a norm be negative? No. Zero for the zero vector, positive for every other vector.

Common misconception

“The norm of a vector is its biggest entry.” That is one norm, but not the L1 or L2 norm. For L2 every entry contributes, and for L1 every entry contributes its size.
