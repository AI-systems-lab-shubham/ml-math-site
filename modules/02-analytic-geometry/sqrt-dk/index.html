Why attention scores are divided by the square root of d_k

Adding more terms makes a dot product spread out; dividing by √dk exactly undoes that, under a stated assumption.

01 Meaning

Take a query vector q and a key vector k, each with dk entries, and compute q·k. It is a sum of dk products. The more terms you add, the more room the sum has to wander, so the typical size of q·k grows with dk. Dividing by the right amount keeps the scores the same size however many entries there are. To find that amount, we need four ideas, each in one sentence:

  • The mean is the average value: the balance point of the numbers.
  • The variance measures how spread out numbers are around their mean: the average of the squared distances from it.
  • Two random numbers are independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the other, like two separate coin flips.
  • The square root of a number is the number that multiplies by itself to give it, so √64 = ….

The assumption: every entry of q and k is independent of the others, with mean 0 and variance 1. Then each product qᵢkᵢ has mean 0 and variance 1, variances of independent numbers add, so q·k has mean 0 and variance dk. Dividing by √dk divides the variance by dk and gives variance 1.

Var(q·k) = dk, Var(q·k / √dk) = 1

03 Real life

  • A walker who takes d steps of 1 metre, each forwards or backwards at random: after d steps the typical distance from the start grows like √d, not like d.
  • Adding the independent errors of d measurements: the typical total error grows like √d, not d.
  • Noise from many independent small sources in a circuit: the total noise grows like the square root of the number of sources.

Recognition rule. If you add d independent wobbles of the same size, the spread of the total is √d times one wobble’s, because variances add.

Spot it. A walker takes many steps of equal size, each forwards or backwards by a coin flip. How does the typical distance from the start grow with the number of steps?

05 Why and when

Without the division, scores get more spread out as dk grows, so some scores are very large in size. A model with a different dk would then have scores of a different size for no good reason. Dividing by √dk keeps the size steady.

When it does not hold. The result needs the assumption: independent entries with mean 0 and variance 1. If each entry instead has variance 4, the variance of q·k is dk × 4 × 4: for dk = 16 that is …, and dividing by √16 does not bring it back to 1. Trained queries and keys are made by learned matrices; nothing here says they have unit variance or independent entries, so this is the argument for why the divisor is a sensible default, not a measurement of a trained model.

06 How to arrive at it

✓ Derived

  1. The requirement. The size of the scores should not depend on dk.
  2. The spread. Under the assumption, each product qᵢkᵢ has variance 1 and q·k adds dk independent such products, so its variance is dk.
  3. The fix. Dividing a number by c divides its variance by c². We want to divide dk by something to get 1, so c² = dk and c = √dk.
  4. The alternatives fail. Dividing by dk itself over-corrects: the variance becomes 1/dk, and scores shrink towards zero as dk grows. Dividing by √dk is the one choice that gives exactly 1.

Before you continue, predict: if we divided by dk instead of √dk, would the variance of the scores be bigger or smaller than 1?

I have made my prediction: show the reasoning

Smaller: dividing by dk divides the variance by dk², giving dk / dk² = 1/dk. For dk = 64 that is far below 1, so the scores would be squeezed towards zero.

07 In an LLM

  • In the original Transformer. The dot products of queries and keys are divided by √dk, with dk = … in the base model, so the divisor is … (Vaswani et al., 2017, §3.2.1). The paper’s footnote gives the argument of this card: if the components of q and k are independent random variables with mean 0 and variance 1, then q·k has mean 0 and variance dk.
  • The paper’s motivation. For large dk the dot products grow large in size, which pushes the step that turns a token’s scores into positive weights adding up to 1 (the softmax) into regions where its slope is tiny, so learning is slow. Dividing by √dk counteracts this.
  • A check you can run. With … random pairs at dk = … and a fixed seed, the variance of q·k came out as … for normal random entries and … for ±1 entries, near …; after dividing by … it was … and …, near 1.

Simplified. The simulation draws entries that really are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, which is the assumption, not a property of trained queries and keys. This card makes no claim about the values inside a trained model.

Teach it

Everyday analogy

Many independent coin flips of ±1 metre: the more flips, the further you typically drift, but only like the square root of the number of flips. Where it breaks down: real queries and keys are not coin flips; the argument holds only under its assumption.

Common misconception

“The scores have variance 1 in a trained model.” The card only shows that dividing by √dk gives variance 1 when the entries are independent with mean 0 and variance 1. Whether trained queries and keys are like that is a separate question.
